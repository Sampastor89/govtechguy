An investment firm recently put out a research request, routed through an expert network, looking to talk to someone with hands-on experience with "AI tools used in government." They wanted to understand the competitive landscape, how the products actually differ, and where the use cases are headed. The vendor list they'd assembled: Vulcan, FiscalNote, Quorum, and Bloomberg Government.

It's a reasonable-looking list. It's also two different markets wearing one label.

---

## Two Buyers, Not One Market

FiscalNote, Quorum, and Bloomberg Government sell to the same buyer: a company, trade association, or advocacy group that needs to watch what government is doing — track bills, flag regulatory risk, coordinate a lobbying response. That's government-affairs intelligence software. It's a mature category with real incumbents, real revenue, and a growing AI feature set layered on top of a data moat that already existed.

Vulcan sells to a completely different buyer: the government itself. Not a company watching an agency — the agency. That's a different product, a different sales motion, and by any honest measure, a different market.

Lumping them together isn't just imprecise. It's the kind of category confusion that makes a market look bigger and more coherent than it actually is — which is exactly the moment worth looking closely at each company on its own.

## FiscalNote: Building the Right Product While the Balance Sheet Falls Apart

FiscalNote went public via SPAC in 2022 and has spent 2026 unwinding. In Q2, revenue came in at **$19.6 million**, down **16%** from the year before, and the company guided full-year 2026 revenue to **$74–76 million** after selling off its FrontierView business unit.<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> In April, the NYSE delisted the stock for trading below the $1.00 minimum for 30 consecutive days; it now trades on the OTCID Basic Market, and the delisting triggered default clauses on FiscalNote's convertible notes that creditors have only agreed to forbear through August.<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>

Here's what makes FiscalNote's story worth more than one sentence of schadenfreude: the product work underneath the stock chart looks legitimate. The company finished migrating its entire customer base onto PolicyNote, its AI-native platform, shipping more than 35 major features in 2025 alone — AI legislative drafting, bill comparison, social listening for early policy signals, AI-generated tariff impact reports.<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup> It's also reporting early enterprise traction on an agentic API product.<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup> FiscalNote is a company doing the right thing on product roadmap while its capital structure collapses around it — a reminder that "is the AI good" and "is the business healthy" are separate questions, and a research client asking only about the former will miss the latter entirely.

## Quorum: Private, Funded, Betting Narrow

Quorum — founded in 2014 by Alex Wirth and Jonathan Marks, headquartered in Washington, DC, and not to be confused with the Houston energy-software company of the same name — has raised roughly **$40.9 million** and serves more than **2,000 organizations**, with over half of the Fortune 100 on its platform.<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup> In July 2026, it took a strategic growth investment from Enlightenment Capital specifically earmarked to expand agentic AI capabilities.<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>

Quorum's AI assistant, Quincy, handles policy intelligence, bill analysis, summarization, meeting notes, internal messaging, and grassroots content generation.<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup> The strategic bet is explicit: rather than bolt on a general-purpose chatbot, Quorum says it's building "purpose-specific intelligence tools for government affairs professionals" — narrow utility over broad capability.<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup> It's the opposite capital story from FiscalNote: private, growth-funded, and under no pressure to manage a public stock price while it ships.

## Bloomberg Government: The Incumbent That Doesn't Need to Raise Anything

Bloomberg Government launched in 2011 as the public-policy arm of Bloomberg Industry Group.<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup> It has no funding round to announce because it doesn't need one — it's backed by Bloomberg's balance sheet and, more importantly, by proprietary editorial content from CQ and Roll Call that neither FiscalNote nor Quorum can replicate. Its AI layer — search, summarization, text comparison, extraction — is grounded in that same verified legislative and regulatory dataset.<sup><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup> BGOV reportedly prices around **$6,000–$15,000 per seat per year** through an enterprise sales process.<sup><a href="#ref-9">[9]</a></sup>

Bloomberg's advantage was never going to be who ships AI features first. It's the data and journalism underneath those features, built up over more than a decade, that a well-funded startup can't just buy its way into.

## The Fourth Company Doesn't Belong on This List

Vulcan is five months old. It graduated from Y Combinator's Summer 2025 batch and raised a **$10.9 million** seed round co-led by General Catalyst and Cubit Capital.<sup><a href="#ref-10">[10]</a></sup> Founders Tanner Jones, Alek Mekhanik, and Chris Minge — a mix of policy, machine learning, and engineering backgrounds out of Dartmouth and Princeton — are building what they call a "regulatory operating system": an agentic platform that claims to ingest **157 billion records** of law, regulation, and case law across every federal, state, and municipal jurisdiction, then analyze statutory language, draft compliance guidance, and generate proposed regulatory text with citations.<sup><a href="#ref-11">[11]</a></sup>

The traction is the part that should actually catch an investor's attention. In 2026, Virginia Governor Glenn Youngkin issued an executive order requiring every state agency to use Vulcan's technology as part of a push to cut **35%** of the state's regulatory code — after a three-month, $150,000 pilot contract.<sup><a href="#ref-12">[12]</a></sup> That's a sitting governor mandating a five-month-old startup's software across an entire state government. It's also drawn skepticism: Cary Coglianese, who directs the University of Pennsylvania's Penn Program on Regulation, has warned that topline deregulation numbers — fewer pages, fewer words — are easy to inflate and don't necessarily reflect a meaningful reduction in regulatory burden.<sup><a href="#ref-12">[12]</a></sup>

That tension is the whole ballgame for this category. Vulcan isn't selling a research tool to a company that wants to monitor government from the outside. It's selling software that drafts the actual language of regulation, inside the agency that enforces it. The stakes, the accuracy bar, and the accountability questions are not the same ones FiscalNote, Quorum, or Bloomberg Government have to answer.

## The Consolidation That's Already Happening in the Other Market

While Vulcan was still at YC, the government-affairs intelligence category was already doing what mature SaaS categories do when AI features become table stakes: consolidating. Plural, an AI-powered bill-tracking startup backed by Google for Startups alumni and used by organizations like NextGen America, was acquired by SAI360 — a governance, risk, and compliance platform — in December 2025.<sup><a href="#ref-13">[13]</a></sup> Plural's pitch was nearly identical to Quorum's and PolicyNote's: AI-generated summaries, topic categorization, and stakeholder engagement tools layered over legislative tracking data.<sup><a href="#ref-14">[14]</a></sup>

Once every vendor in a category ships the same AI summarization and alerting features on top of broadly similar legislative datasets, differentiation stops being about the model and starts being about distribution, data exclusivity, and who can absorb a smaller competitor. That's precisely the dynamic that's already reshaped CIS billing software and public-safety software in this market — and it's arriving in government-affairs intelligence on the same schedule.

## Where This Actually Goes

The research client's instinct to look at "AI for government" as one emerging category is understandable — the phrase is everywhere right now. But the four companies on that list are proof it's splitting into two markets that will behave nothing alike.

Government-affairs intelligence — FiscalNote, Quorum, Bloomberg Government, and whatever's left of Plural inside SAI360 — is a mature, consolidating SaaS category. The AI features are converging fast across vendors, which means the next few years of competition get decided by data moats and balance sheets, not roadmaps. FiscalNote's situation is the cautionary tale: good product, broken capital structure, and a reminder that being right about AI doesn't save a company from being wrong about everything else.

Agency-side AI — Vulcan, for now mostly alone — is a different animal entirely: pre-seed-stage companies making existential, state-by-state bets on whether a governor or agency head will trust unproven software with the literal text of the law. It's a tiny market today. It's also the one where a single executive order can turn a five-month-old startup into critical infrastructure overnight, for better or worse.

An investor pricing these as one opportunity is going to misprice both halves. One is a margin-compression story. The other is a binary bet on institutional trust that hasn't been tested yet at scale. Good research starts by refusing to accept the four-company list as given — and asking who's actually buying.
