Customer Information Systems — the software that meters, bills, and collects payment from every household's water, gas, electric, and sewer service — are among the least glamorous categories in government technology.<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> They are also, increasingly, one of the most contested. Behind the flat, utilitarian branding of vendors most residents will never hear of, a genuine consolidation wave is underway: four of the seven most prominent CIS providers in the municipal market have changed ownership or taken major growth capital since early 2024.<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>

That is not a coincidence. It is the vertical-SaaS playbook — proven in tax software, courts software, and student information systems — arriving at utility billing.<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>

## Market Size and Growth Trajectory

Estimates of the CIS market's size vary considerably depending on scope. Reports that isolate core CIS software put the 2026 market between $1.65 billion and $2.46 billion, growing at a CAGR of roughly 8.8% to 16.5%, and reaching an estimated $3.26 billion by 2030.<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup> Broader definitions that fold in adjacent billing functionality put the 2026 figure closer to $6.6–6.7 billion, expanding to nearly $9.9 billion by 2031.<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup> Narrowed specifically to the municipal segment — the piece of the market this piece is focused on — one estimate sizes it at roughly $2 billion in 2025, growing at 8% annually to $3.5 billion by 2033.<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>

North America holds the largest current regional share of the CIS market, at roughly 37–39%, driven by a wave of legacy-system replacement.<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup> Asia-Pacific is growing fastest, at 14–15% CAGR, propelled by smart-city buildouts rather than incumbent displacement.<sup><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup>

The addressable opportunity sits almost entirely in the long tail. Investor-owned utilities serve the majority of U.S. electricity customers and run large, complex CIS deployments — typically Oracle, SAP, or enterprise-grade Harris systems.<sup><a href="#ref-9">[9]</a></sup> Municipal and public power utilities, by contrast, are numerous, small, and disproportionately still running legacy or homegrown billing systems — the exact profile that cloud-native challengers are built to target.<sup><a href="#ref-9">[9]</a></sup>

## What's Actually Changing

**Cloud migration is now table stakes, not a differentiator.** Hundreds of small and mid-sized utilities are still running CIS platforms designed before cloud infrastructure, smart metering, or mobile payment existed.<sup><a href="#ref-10">[10]</a></sup> A mid-sized municipality with 5,000–15,000 accounts should expect to spend $50,000–$150,000 on a basic modern SaaS deployment, with larger implementations running $300,000–$500,000.<sup><a href="#ref-10">[10]</a></sup> The biggest barrier isn't budget — it's that public-sector IT teams often lack in-house cloud security and cost-management expertise.<sup><a href="#ref-11">[11]</a></sup>

**AMI data is an underexploited asset.** The global advanced metering infrastructure market grew from roughly $9.3 billion in 2021 toward a projected $20.4 billion by 2026.<sup><a href="#ref-12">[12]</a></sup> But most of that smart-meter data still lands in a meter data management layer, powers a bill, and then goes quiet — rather than feeding proactive leak alerts, time-of-use rates, or demand response.<sup><a href="#ref-13">[13]</a></sup> Closing that gap is one of the clearest differentiators available to modern CIS vendors right now.

**Self-service has crossed from nice-to-have to expected.** Mobile app satisfaction scores now run 41 points higher than desktop experiences in J.D. Power's utility digital experience research — yet 28% of the largest U.S. utilities still don't offer a mobile app at all.<sup><a href="#ref-14">[14]</a></sup> Sixty-eight percent of consumers paid a bill via mobile device in the past year.<sup><a href="#ref-15">[15]</a></sup> Pay-by-text adoption grew fivefold year-over-year, and integrated self-service platforms are reporting call-center volume reductions of up to 68%.<sup><a href="#ref-16">[16]</a></sup>

**Interoperability is replacing rigid, monthly-cycle architecture.** As distributed energy resources, EV charging, and new service models proliferate, CIS platforms are shifting from meter-centric batch processing toward event-driven, API-first "service-to-cash" architecture.<sup><a href="#ref-17">[17]</a></sup> "True interoperability" via open APIs is now standard marketing language across the category.<sup><a href="#ref-18">[18]</a></sup>

**AI has arrived in the call center and the collections queue.** Oracle added AI-powered call summarization to its Utilities Customer Platform in May 2025.<sup><a href="#ref-19">[19]</a></sup> In collections specifically, AI tools are being used to detect customer distress signals, trigger empathetic escalation, and optimize whether to reach a customer by voice, SMS, email, or chat.<sup><a href="#ref-20">[20]</a></sup>

**PCI compliance remains the dominant regulatory driver.** Any utility accepting card payments is bound by PCI-DSS, enforced contractually through acquiring banks rather than statute.<sup><a href="#ref-21">[21]</a></sup> Increasingly, municipalities offload that risk entirely to PCI Level-1 payment partners rather than carrying compliance burden in-house.<sup><a href="#ref-22">[22]</a></sup>

## Market Structure: A Fragmented Category With a Concentrated Top

The global CIS market has an estimated 45 active vendors, with the top five — Oracle, SAP, Hansen Technologies, Gentrack, and Itineris — holding roughly 58% combined share.<sup><a href="#ref-23">[23]</a></sup> Below that tier, segmentation is driven primarily by utility size rather than incumbency alone: large utilities gravitate toward enterprise-scale systems, mid-to-large utilities toward vendors like VertexOne, and the small-to-mid municipal tier remains the primary battleground where cloud-native challengers compete for legacy replacements.<sup><a href="#ref-24">[24]</a></sup>

Buyer behavior reinforces this split. Investor-owned utilities carry larger IT budgets and gravitate toward enterprise or ERP-integrated CIS. Municipal and public utilities are more price-constrained and procurement-driven, favoring SaaS subscription pricing and vendors with strong government reference lists — a structural reality that shapes nearly every vendor's go-to-market strategy in this category.

## How Municipalities Actually Buy This Software

Procurement in this market is slow by design. A full cycle from initial budget conversation to contract award typically runs 12–18 months, with the formal RFP phase alone taking 3–6 months and RFPs commonly posted for 30–90 days.<sup><a href="#ref-25">[25]</a></sup> Cooperative purchasing vehicles — state contracts and multi-jurisdiction purchasing agreements — can compress that timeline to 4–6 weeks for vendors already on an approved contract, which is a real go-to-market advantage for incumbents and a real barrier for newer entrants.<sup><a href="#ref-26">[26]</a></sup>

Once awarded, implementation costs for a CIS deployment run $500,000 to $5 million depending on utility size and customization, and more than 70% of implementations experience cost or timeline overruns — most often because of underestimated data migration effort.<sup><a href="#ref-27">[27]</a></sup> That single fact does a lot to explain why switching costs remain high even as legacy systems age out. But that moat is eroding: as legacy vendors slow their pace of patching, integrating those platforms with modern AMI and payment systems becomes progressively harder, creating a forcing function toward migration even for utilities with no proactive modernization plan.<sup><a href="#ref-28">[28]</a></sup>

## The Consolidation Wave, Company by Company

This is where the market's structural story becomes concrete. Of seven prominent CIS vendors serving the municipal market, four have changed ownership or taken major PE/growth capital in the past two years alone.

### Tyler Technologies — the horizontal incumbent

Tyler (NYSE: TYL) is the only public, standalone company in this group, with FY2025 revenue of roughly $2.33 billion companywide.<sup><a href="#ref-29">[29]</a></sup> Utility billing is a module inside Tyler's Munis/Enterprise ERP suite rather than a standalone product line, which is both its strength and its limitation: more than 1,100 utilities use Tyler's utility applications, largely because they want billing bundled into one system alongside finance, HR, and procurement.<sup><a href="#ref-30">[30]</a></sup> Tyler was named a Leader in Gartner's 2025 Magic Quadrant for Cloud-Based ERP for U.S. Local Government.<sup><a href="#ref-31">[31]</a></sup> Review data tells a more mixed story on the utility billing module specifically — praised for payment-site reliability, criticized on Capterra as "clunky" and hard to self-learn.<sup><a href="#ref-32">[32]</a></sup> Tyler wins on breadth; it is not winning on best-in-class utility billing UX.

### Harris (N. Harris Computer Corporation) — the roll-up playbook, run at scale

Harris, a private subsidiary of Constellation Software (TSX: CSU), doesn't compete with one product — it owns roughly ten separately branded, independently operated CIS companies, including Advanced Utility Systems, NorthStar, Cayenta, Cogsdale, Systems & Software, and the European operator SIV.AG.<sup><a href="#ref-33">[33]</a></sup> This is Constellation's classic strategy: acquire, keep the acquired brand operating independently, and let each business serve a different segment — NorthStar for small-to-mid utilities, Systems & Software and Advanced Utility Systems across municipal, investor-owned, and cooperative utilities.<sup><a href="#ref-34">[34]</a></sup> The tradeoff is disclosure: because Harris runs a low-marketing, decentralized model, there is no aggregate customer count, no utilities-segment revenue figure, and minimal public review footprint across the portfolio — a stark contrast to Tyler's single, heavily reviewed brand.<sup><a href="#ref-35">[35]</a></sup>

### SpryPoint — the well-funded pure-play challenger

SpryPoint has taken the opposite path from Harris: one unified, cloud-native, utility-only platform (SpryCIS, plus modules for self-service, payments, field service, and AMI data management), with no ERP ambitions of its own.<sup><a href="#ref-36">[36]</a></sup> Founded in Charlottetown, Prince Edward Island in 2012, the company has grown to roughly 285 employees and crossed 100 utility clients across the Americas as of 2025–2026.<sup><a href="#ref-37">[37]</a></sup> It received an initial investment from Norwest Venture Partners in 2023 and a strategic growth investment from Insight Partners that closed in December 2025.<sup><a href="#ref-38">[38]</a></sup> Its 2026 win list is notable: the City of Missoula, the City of Lewisville, Irvine Ranch Water District (paired with Workday for ERP), and a multi-jurisdiction win through the LOGIS purchasing cooperative in Minnesota, whose executive director stated bluntly that SpryPoint "was the only product that would replace our legacy system."<sup><a href="#ref-39">[39]</a></sup>

### Muni-Link Software — acquired into an industrial giant

Muni-Link, a Pennsylvania-based CIS provider with roots in Link Computer Corporation, was acquired by Roper Technologies (NYSE: ROP) for $118 million in February 2025 and folded into Roper's Neptune Technology Group, its meter-hardware business.<sup><a href="#ref-40">[40]</a></sup> That pairing is strategically obvious: Neptune already serves more than 4,000 water utilities with hardware, and Muni-Link now gives Roper a billing-software cross-sell into that installed base.<sup><a href="#ref-41">[41]</a></sup> It's also, by a meaningful margin, the best-reviewed vendor in this group — a 4.8 out of 5 rating across 59 Capterra reviews, with customers specifically praising responsive support and easy training.<sup><a href="#ref-42">[42]</a></sup> Being acquired by a large public industrial company should mean better-funded R&D going forward; it also raises the standard post-acquisition question of how much product-roadmap independence survives the integration.

### Edmunds GovTech — the acquisitive platform play

Edmunds GovTech is structurally different from the other niche players: it's a broad, 26-module local-government ERP suite, with utility billing as one component rather than the whole product.<sup><a href="#ref-43">[43]</a></sup> It has been PE-backed since 2018 (LLR Partners), with TA Associates joining as a co-investor in April 2024, and has spent that capital acquiring smaller regional CIS vendors — Municipal Software, Inc. in 2022, and RosTech, Inc. in February 2025 — to expand its footprint, which now spans more than 2,100 municipalities across 20-plus states.<sup><a href="#ref-44">[44]</a></sup> Edmunds is arguably the clearest evidence of the roll-up thesis in this market: rather than out-building competitors, it is buying them.

### Springbrook Software — three owners in a decade

Springbrook's ownership history is the most turbulent of the seven: founded in 1985, acquired by Accela in 2015, carved out by Accel-KKR in 2020, and sold by Accel-KKR to Five Arrows — the alternative-assets arm of Rothschild & Co. — in June 2024.<sup><a href="#ref-45">[45]</a></sup> Like Edmunds, its flagship Cirrus platform is a broad local-government ERP (finance, HR, payroll, budgeting, utility billing) rather than a pure-play CIS product, and it earned a spot in Gartner's inaugural Magic Quadrant for Cloud-Based ERP for U.S. Local Government in May 2025 alongside Tyler, Oracle, SAP, and Workday.<sup><a href="#ref-46">[46]</a></sup> A new CEO, Chris Dinkler, took over in December 2025 with an explicit mandate to accelerate growth and launch an "AI-driven Smart City initiative."<sup><a href="#ref-47">[47]</a></sup>

### Continental Utility Solutions — the last independent standing

CUSI is the outlier in every sense. Privately held, headquartered in Jonesboro, Arkansas, and tracing back to 1984 — pioneering PC-based utility billing when mainframes still dominated the category — it has no PE ownership, no acquisition history, and no acquirer in its own future that public sources have surfaced.<sup><a href="#ref-48">[48]</a></sup> With an estimated 76 employees and a product line (UB4, UMS) that has modernized onto Windows/SQL/.NET with a hosted cloud option, CUSI is competing on its own against companies backed by Insight Partners, Roper, TA Associates, and Constellation Software.<sup><a href="#ref-49">[49]</a></sup> Whether that independence is a differentiator or a vulnerability is, in a market this actively consolidating, an open question.

## What the Consolidation Pattern Actually Means

Four of seven vendors — Muni-Link, Edmunds GovTech, Springbrook, and SpryPoint — have changed hands or taken significant capital since early 2024. That's not noise; it's the same vertical-SaaS roll-up dynamic playing out across the broader software market, where 2,698 SaaS M&A deals closed in 2025 alone, an all-time high fueled by roughly $3.7 trillion in global private-equity dry powder.<sup><a href="#ref-50">[50]</a></sup>

For municipal buyers, this means procurement decisions increasingly double as bets on capital structure, not just product fit. A CIS platform backed by a well-capitalized sponsor may get faster roadmap investment — or may face pricing pressure and roadmap consolidation once that sponsor looks for an exit. For vendors like CUSI, remaining independent is either a genuine point of differentiation with buyers wary of PE-driven price increases, or a structural disadvantage against rivals with meaningfully deeper R&D budgets. Either way, the CIS market's next chapter is being written less in product demos than in term sheets.
